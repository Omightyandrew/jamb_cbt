Comprehension: read for meaning and evidence

Use a consistent process instead of rereading the whole passage after every question.

1

Read for the passage's structure

Notice the topic, the writer's purpose, and how each paragraph contributes. A passage may explain, narrate, describe, or argue.

2

Identify what the question asks

Separate a question about a stated detail from one asking for an inference, tone, purpose, or word meaning.

3

Locate evidence before selecting

Return to the relevant sentence or paragraph. The best answer should fit the text, not just sound plausible from general knowledge.

4

Check every option against the evidence

Eliminate choices that reverse a detail, overstate the writer's claim, or introduce information the passage does not support.

Inference, tone, and author's purpose

Inference stays close to the text

An inference is a conclusion supported by details in the passage. It should not rely on a belief or fact that the writer never introduced.

Tone comes from language choices

Look at descriptive words, contrasts, and the writer's attitude before choosing a tone label. Do not infer tone from the topic alone.

Purpose explains why the passage is written

Ask whether the writer is mainly informing, explaining, narrating, persuading, or describing, and support the choice with textual clues.

Summary and synthesis

A useful summary preserves essential ideas without inserting commentary.

Find the points that answer the task

Use the exact question or instruction to decide which points matter; a general retelling may include too much detail.

Combine related ideas carefully

Group points that express the same theme, but keep distinctions that change the meaning.

Use concise wording

Prefer clear, direct language. Check that the summary retains the passage's meaning and does not add an opinion.

Lexis and structure: use context and grammar

Vocabulary in context

Read the whole sentence and nearby clues before choosing a meaning. The same word can have different meanings in different contexts.

Original example: “The judge dismissed the claim as frivolous.” Here, frivolous means not serious or sensible, rather than merely unusual.

Synonyms, antonyms, and idioms

Check whether a proposed synonym or opposite preserves the sentence's intended meaning. Interpret idioms and figurative expressions by their conventional or contextual sense, not word by word.

Concord and sentence completion

Find the main subject before choosing a verb. Check tense, pronouns, prepositions, and linking words so the completed sentence makes grammatical and logical sense.

Original example: “Neither Ada nor her sister is late.” Both coordinated subjects are singular in this sentence, so the singular verb fits.

Sentence interpretation

Preserve the relationship between clauses, qualifiers, and contrast words.

Original example: “Although it rained, the match continued.” The word although signals contrast: the match went ahead despite the rain. It does not say that rain caused the match to continue.

When comparing possible interpretations, restate the sentence in simpler words and check that the restatement keeps the same meaning.

Oral forms and pronunciation

In sound questions, spelling is not a reliable substitute for listening to or representing the sound.

Practise sound contrasts

Review vowel and consonant distinctions using the pronunciation conventions in your current syllabus and study materials.

Mark stress before answering

For a stress task, separate a word into syllables and identify which syllable receives emphasis. Do not assume that related words share the same stress.

Illustration: In common English pronunciation, REcord (noun) and reCORD (verb) differ in stress. Check the pronunciation model used in your course.

Review rhyme and intonation

Compare the relevant sounds at the ends of words for rhyme, and pay attention to how emphasis and voice movement affect spoken meaning.

A repeatable practice and error-review routine

  1. 1

    Choose one skill

    For example, inference, concord, contextual vocabulary, or stress.

  2. 2

    Attempt a short set carefully

    For each item, state what evidence or rule you used before checking the answer.

  3. 3

    Classify mistakes

    Record whether the problem was comprehension, grammar, vocabulary, pronunciation, or careless reading.

  4. 4

    Retry after review

    Study the missing concept, then attempt a fresh example to see whether you can apply the reasoning.

Frequently asked questions

How should I answer comprehension inference questions?

Find the relevant passage evidence, combine it with only the reasoning needed, and reject options that add unsupported information.

What does lexis and structure mean?

It concerns vocabulary and how words and sentence structures work in context. Confirm the precise current scope in the official syllabus.

How can I practise oral forms?

Practise sound contrasts, syllable stress, rhyme, emphasis, and intonation using the pronunciation conventions in your syllabus and learning materials.

Should I memorise long synonym lists?

Build vocabulary, but practise choosing meanings in context because a word's best equivalent depends on how it is used.
